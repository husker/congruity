**Synthesis claim:** P15's submodular selection theory + P14's density-peak clustering + P1's learned solver-decision framing gives a benchmark-curation method for instance-adaptive portfolios: cluster route instances by solver-relevant behavior, then choose a diverse, budget-constrained representative subset for training learned solver-control policies. No single paper provides both unsupervised regime discovery, diversity-constrained subset selection, and the solver-learning target.

**Compatibility argument:** Density-peak clustering can identify candidate regimes and outliers; a monotone submodular objective under a matroid constraint can encode coverage of regimes, diversity, and labeling or runtime-budget constraints; the selected subset then trains or evaluates the learned solver decisions described by P1. The methods compose as data curation plus downstream learning.

**Constituent papers:**

- **P15** — role: budgeted diverse subset selection
  - dimensions: representative_subset_selection
  - quote: _"the greedy algorithm yields a $(1-1/e)$-approximation."_
- **P14** — role: instance-regime and outlier discovery
  - dimensions: instance_space_clustering
  - quote: _"centers have high local density and large distance from denser points."_
- **P1** — role: downstream learned solver control
  - dimensions: learned_solver_decisions
  - quote: _"pushing further the integration of machine learning and combinatorial optimization."_

**Covered dimensions:** representative_subset_selection, instance_space_clustering, learned_solver_decisions

**Open questions:** What matroid captures benchmark budget and per-regime coverage constraints?; Should subset utility use features, solver traces, or both?; Does curated training improve generalization to unseen route regimes?

**Codex confidence:** 8/10 — _Strong modular data-selection synthesis, although it needs route-specific features from outside these three papers._

**Verification:**

- L1 internal consistency: **PASS**
- L2 source grounding: **FAIL**

- L3 adversarial check: **WEAK** — The composition is plausible as a data-curation pipeline, but its strongest claim is much weaker than advertised: none of the cited papers establishes that unsupervised density clusters in route-instance feature space align with solver-performance regimes, and without solver-runtime labels the method may select visually diverse but portfolio-irrelevant instances. This is not logically impossible; a benchmark could show that density-peak-plus-submodular curation improves learned solver control versus random, stratified, active-learning, and instance-space-analysis baselines. But until that experiment exists, the proposal is an underspecified and likely incremental combination of known clustering, diverse subset selection, and algorithm-selection ideas.
  - [challenge] Solver-relevance is assumed, not obtained: Density-peak clustering discovers high-density structure under a chosen similarity metric. The composition needs clusters by solver-relevant behavior, which is a much stronger object: instances that induce similar runtime, failure modes, branch-and-bound behavior, SAT encodings, or heuristic trajectories. P14 does not provide that metric, and if the metric is built from solver-performance vectors then the expensive labels the method was supposed to economize have already been collected. This is the central hidden gap.
  - [challenge] Budget constraint mismatch: P15 is about monotone submodular maximization under a matroid constraint. A realistic route-instance labeling budget is usually knapsack-like because runtime and annotation costs vary sharply by instance and solver. Cardinality or partition matroids can model fixed counts per regime, but not arbitrary heterogeneous runtime budgets. The composition gestures at budget constraints without showing that its feasible set is actually a matroid or that the chosen approximation guarantee still applies.
  - [challenge] P1 contributes only a broad framing: P1 is a methodological survey arguing that optimization instances can be treated as data points and that distributions matter for learning. It does not provide a concrete learned solver-control target, architecture, feature map, loss, or validation protocol for TSP, Hamiltonian Cycle, or VRP portfolios. The composition therefore leaves the hardest downstream step underdefined: what policy is trained, what decisions it controls, and how performance attribution separates better curation from ordinary solver-selection learning.
  - [challenge] Likely folklore rather than discovery: Clustering instances, selecting representative or diverse subsets, and training algorithm-selection or portfolio models is already a natural combination in automated algorithm configuration, per-instance algorithm selection, active learning, and instance-space analysis. Submodular facility-location-style objectives are also standard for dataset summarization. The proposed bridge may be useful, but the novelty claim is fragile unless it beats obvious baselines and distinguishes itself from existing representative-instance selection workflows.
  - [challenge] Unsupervised regime discovery can suppress rare hard cases: Density-based methods privilege dense regions and treat low-density points as outliers. In hard routing portfolios, the most important instances may be exactly rare pathological structures: near-Hamiltonian traps, metric violations, clustered demands, tight time windows, or symmetry-heavy graphs. Excluding or downweighting outliers may improve average representativeness while damaging the learned controller on the instances where portfolio adaptation matters most.
  - [challenge] Objective design may not be submodular: Coverage and diversity terms can often be made submodular, but solver-learning utility need not be. Training-set value for a learned policy can have complementarities: two regimes together may reveal a decision boundary that neither reveals alone, or a rare contrast pair may be valuable only jointly. If the real utility is not approximately submodular, P15's guarantee applies only to a proxy objective, not to the actual portfolio performance objective.
  - [challenge] No protection against distribution shift: The method curates a subset from an available instance pool, but the problem asks for instance-adaptive portfolios on hard route problems generally. If the pool is generated by a narrow benchmark family, clustering and submodular selection can faithfully summarize the wrong distribution. P1 explicitly raises the importance of the relevant problem distribution, but the composition does not specify how that distribution is defined, sampled, stress-tested, or updated.

### C5 — 3 papers — verified at: internal_consistency

**Synthesis claim:** P4's graph-limit perspective + P5's GNN representation learning + P14's density-peak clustering creates a route-instance taxonomy for large benchmark collections: represent each instance with learned graph embeddings, compare them to asymptotic structural regimes, and cluster them to expose families where different solvers may dominate. No single paper combines large-graph structural characterization, neural graph embeddings, and unsupervised instance-space discovery.

**Compatibility argument:** Graph-limit ideas provide structural coordinates or hypotheses for large route graphs; GNNs provide finite-instance embeddings; density-peak clustering can operate over either learned embeddings, graph-limit-inspired features, or both. The synthesis does not require the clustering method to know graph theory internally, only that graph-derived features are measurable.

**Constituent papers:**

**Covered dimensions:** graph_structural_features, learned_graph_representations, instance_space_clustering

**Open questions:** Are route instances large enough for graph-limit features to be meaningful?; Do learned embeddings preserve solver-relevant asymptotic structure?; Can clusters predict portfolio choice rather than just visual similarity?

**Codex confidence:** 6/10 — _Conceptually coherent for large instance sets, but graph-limit features may be too coarse for many benchmark route problems._

**Verification:**

- L1 internal consistency: **PASS**
- L2 source grounding: **FAIL**
  - P4: VERBATIM_MATCH
  - P5: QUOTE_NOT_IN_ABSTRACT
  - P14: VERBATIM_MATCH
- L3 adversarial check: **WEAK** — The composition is plausible as an empirical instance-space analysis project, but it does not yet solve the stated solver-portfolio problem. Its main weakness is that it substitutes an unsupervised taxonomy for the critical missing link: evidence that clusters predict solver dominance, transfer across benchmark distributions, and add value beyond standard instance features plus algorithm-selection baselines. No theorem or direct contradiction makes it impossible, so a definitive benchmark with solver-performance labels could rescue it.
  - [challenge] Missing portfolio-selection step: The proposal clusters route instances but does not specify how clusters become actionable solver choices. Solver portfolios require performance-labeled instances, selection/regression policies, uncertainty handling, and validation against algorithm-selection baselines. A taxonomy can look coherent while having no predictive relationship to which exact, heuristic, SAT, or IP solver wins.
  - [challenge] Graph-limit mismatch: Classical graph-limit theory is strongest for asymptotic structural behavior of large graph sequences, often unweighted or dense settings. Hard route benchmarks are frequently metric, weighted, geometric, sparse, constrained, or include vehicle capacities and time windows. The graphon-style structural coordinates may collapse precisely the weights, geometry, and side constraints that determine solver hardness.
  - [challenge] GNN embeddings need a training signal: A GNN does not automatically produce solver-relevant instance embeddings. If trained self-supervised, embeddings may encode superficial graph statistics; if trained on solver performance, the method is no longer an unsupervised taxonomy but a supervised algorithm-selection model. The composition does not state the objective that aligns embeddings with hardness or solver dominance.
  - [challenge] Density-peak clustering is generic, not a discovery engine: Density-peak clustering can partition almost any embedding space, but high-dimensional learned embeddings often have unstable density estimates and distance concentration. The resulting clusters may be artifacts of scaling, embedding dimension, or cutoff choice rather than meaningful route-instance families.
  - [challenge] Novelty is overstated: The broad idea of instance-space analysis, clustering benchmark instances, learning graph representations, and mapping regions to solver performance is already close to established algorithm-selection and per-instance algorithm-configuration practice. The claimed novelty of combining graph limits, GNNs, and density peaks may be mostly packaging unless it yields a measurable improvement over simpler handcrafted features, PCA/UMAP, random forests, or SATzilla-style selectors.
  - [challenge] Benchmark-family leakage: Large route-problem collections often contain generator-specific fingerprints. A learned embedding plus clustering pipeline may rediscover benchmark source, graph size, or Euclidean/non-Euclidean origin rather than latent solver-relevant structure. That would make the taxonomy brittle on new real-world instances.
  - [challenge] Constituent papers support weaker claims: P4 supplies a mathematical framework for large-network limits, P5 surveys GNN architectures, and P14 gives a generic clustering heuristic. None of them establishes that graph-limit coordinates are recoverable from finite routing instances, that GNN embeddings preserve solver-relevant asymptotic structure, or that density peaks reveal clusters aligned with algorithm performance.
